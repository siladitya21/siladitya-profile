13.1 Introduction

Project based learning gives thorough practical exposure to a real problem. Projects are usually done in groups, where students practice working together, problem-solving, decision-making and investigation. The underlying process is always the same: analyse the problem, break it into smaller modules, apply a method to solve each module, then integrate the module solutions into the complete answer — gathering and processing the relevant data along the way, and reporting results in a predetermined format.

13.2 Approaches for Solving Projects

A modular (structured) approach divides a project into manageable modules, each with a well-defined task, a set of inputs, and a set of outputs — integrating all the module outputs produces the overall outcome. Five steps carry a project from idea to result:

Figure 13.1 — the project lifecycle
1. IDENTIFICATION OF A PROJECT
   The idea can come from any real-life situation. Understand its
   usefulness and impact; interdisciplinary projects are encouraged.

2. DEFINING A PLAN
   Identify a project leader; define everyone's role clearly;
   assign specific activities and the tools needed for each;
   think through edge/extreme cases for a better solution.

3. FIXING A TIME FRAME AND PROCESSING
   Every activity needs an estimated duration. The plan should be
   well-structured but still FLEXIBLE in its time frame.

4. PROVIDING GUIDANCE AND MONITORING
   When a team gets stuck, seek guidance from books, websites or
   experts. The project leader AND the guide teacher both monitor
   progress.

5. OUTCOME OF A PROJECT
   The result can be single or multiple outputs -- peer-reviewed
   and revised based on feedback from the guide teacher or users.

13.3 Teamwork

Teamwork is the collective effort of individuals accomplishing a task together — the textbook's own example: a bowler's good ball still doesn't take a wicket if the fielder drops the catch. A cricket team needs batting, bowling and fielding all contributing to win.

13.3.1 — Six components of successful teamwork
COMMUNICATE   via email, phone, or group meetings -- helps members
              understand each other and resolve problems
LISTEN        understand others' ideas in meetings; follow agreed steps
SHARE         ideas, images and tools -- whoever has expertise in an
              area should share it with the rest of the team
RESPECT       every member's thoughts deserve consideration; disrespect
              can stop a member from giving their best
HELP          a helping hand (even from outside the team) is often key
PARTICIPATE   every member should actively take part, so each feels
              their importance in the team

13.4 Project Descriptions — three sample briefs from the textbook

These are the textbook's own example projects. A group may pick one of these, or propose a different project to their guide teacher.

Project 1 — Automation of Order Processing in a Restaurant

Brief: A new restaurant, "Stay Healthy," wants software to generate bills and maintain records, automating order placement end to end.

Required functionality
- Two login types: Manager and Customer
- Customer kiosk at reception: shows a menu to place orders
- Customer enters Item Code(s) + quantity
- After ordering: a soft-copy bill with a unique Order Number
  (e.g. date + order number of the day), saved to a data file/database
- Order Number resets to 1 every day
- Manager login: add/change menu items, delete an order on demand
- Manager report: a Sales Summary for a date the user enters
- Add at least ONE more report of your own choice

This maps directly onto Chapter 9 (SQL tables for menu/orders/bills) and Chapter 2 (file handling) or a MySQL backend, with a Python front end for the kiosk/manager screens.

Project 2 — Development of a Puzzle (Minesweeper-style)

Brief: A grid-board game where some cells hide a bomb. Clicking a safe cell reveals a number — a clue about how many bombs are in the adjacent cells. Clicking a bomb ends the game.

Suggested scope
- Fix the grid at 6x6 with exactly 6 bombs, to keep it manageable
- Play 5 rounds of a real Minesweeper game FIRST, before coding,
  to understand the mechanic properly
- Handle exceptions that can arise during play (e.g. invalid cell
  input) -- directly reuses Chapter 1's try/except

Project 3 — Educational Game: "Match the Sum"

Brief: An edutainment game for 5–7 year-olds that teaches summing to 10, using digits that continuously stream into a row of cells.

Required functionality
- Display 15 cells on screen, each holding one digit (1-9)
- A new random digit is generated at regular time intervals and
  inserted at the RIGHTMOST cell; existing digits shift one cell left
- The player types 2 or 3 of the currently-displayed digits (one at
  a time)
- If those digits SUM TO 10, remove them from the cell list
- Game continues until there's no empty cell left to insert a digit
- Handle the case where the chosen digits do NOT sum to 10

This is a natural fit for the Queue/Deque ideas from Chapter 4 (digits entering/leaving a bounded list) plus a simple timer loop.

What comes next
