Week 9: Financials Basics: GL, Journals & Periods

Every invoice, bill and payment from the last two weeks eventually lands in the same place: the general ledger. This week pulls back the curtain on where that posting actually goes, how a journal entry works when a transaction doesn't exist to post it automatically, how accounting periods box time into closeable chunks, and the basics of what changes when more than one currency is involved. None of this is new in NetSuite specifically — it's double-entry bookkeeping — but seeing it as records you can open and read, instead of abstract accounting theory, is what makes the rest of the financial side of the course click.

  • Explain what the chart of accounts is and how transactions post to the general ledger
  • Write a balanced journal entry and describe why an accounting period gets "closed"
  • Describe how multi-currency transactions are recorded and revalued

1. Chart of Accounts & the General Ledger

The chart of accounts is the list of every account a business tracks money in — "Accounts Receivable," "Sales Revenue," "Office Supplies Expense," and so on — each one typed as an Asset, Liability, Equity, Income or Expense account. The general ledger (GL) is the complete record of every debit and credit ever posted against those accounts; every balanced financial transaction in NetSuite — invoices, bills, payments, journal entries — ultimately writes a GL impact behind the scenes.

This is the quiet payoff of Week 1's "one platform" idea: when a sales order is invoiced, there's no separate step where someone "enters it into the books." The invoice is the GL posting. You can open almost any transaction and click through to a "GL Impact" view that shows exactly which accounts were debited and credited, in what amounts — which is the single best way to actually understand how a transaction type affects the books, rather than memorizing it abstractly.

an invoice's GL impact
INVOICE #9001 for $1,200 (Widget A x 10 @ $120)

  GL IMPACT:
    DEBIT   Accounts Receivable      $1,200
    CREDIT  Sales Revenue - Widgets          $1,200
Items point at accounts, not the other way around

Every income/expense item on an item record has a default account tied to it (e.g. "Widget A" posts to "Sales Revenue - Widgets"). That's how NetSuite knows which GL account a transaction line should hit without a human picking an account on every single invoice.

2. Journal Entries, Accounting Periods & Period Close Basics

Not every financial adjustment comes from a normal business transaction — accruals, depreciation, reclassifying an expense between departments, correcting a miscategorized item — these go through a Journal Entry: a direct, manual set of debits and credits against the chart of accounts, with no sales order or vendor bill behind it. Like any GL posting, a journal entry's debits and credits must balance exactly to zero before it can be saved.

An accounting period (typically a calendar month) is the time window a transaction's posting date falls into. Periods move through statuses — Open, Locked (for a specific transaction type), and finally Closed — and once a period is closed, no new transaction can post to it, which is exactly the point: closing a period is how a business "finalizes" a month's books so prior numbers stop shifting under a finance team's feet.

a balanced journal entry (accrual)
JOURNAL ENTRY — Accrue unbilled consulting revenue, March

  DEBIT   Accrued Revenue           $5,000
  CREDIT  Consulting Revenue                 $5,000
  -------------------------------------------------
  Total debits: $5,000   Total credits: $5,000   -> balanced, can save
A closed period isn't necessarily permanent

An administrator can reopen a closed period if a genuine correction is needed, but doing so is treated as a controlled, auditable exception — not a routine action. If closing periods feels easy to undo in your sandbox, remember production finance teams usually restrict that permission tightly on purpose.

3. Multi-Currency Fundamentals

When a customer or vendor transacts in a currency other than the company's base currency, NetSuite records the transaction in the foreign currency but also stores its equivalent in base currency, using the exchange rate in effect at that time. A customer invoiced in EUR by a USD-base-currency company shows both: $1,080 in base currency for internal reporting, €1,000 in the actual currency the customer owes and will pay in.

Because exchange rates move, open foreign-currency balances need periodic revaluation — restating what an open EUR invoice is worth in USD as of today's rate versus the rate on the day it was created. The difference becomes a realized or unrealized gain/loss, which is its own small piece of the period-close process for any company operating across currencies. (Week 24's OneWorld module goes much further into multi-currency consolidation across whole subsidiaries.)

a EUR invoice from a USD-base company
Invoice date:        €1,000 @ 1.08 USD/EUR  = $1,080 (base currency, booked)
Revaluation date:     €1,000 @ 1.05 USD/EUR  = $1,050 (base currency, now)
                                               -----------
                                               -$30 unrealized FX loss
"Unrealized" becomes "realized" the moment cash changes hands

An unrealized FX gain/loss is just a paper revaluation of a still-open balance. Once that EUR invoice is actually paid, the gain or loss becomes realized and permanent — the difference matters for how it flows through the income statement.

4. Hands-on Exercise

Hands-on

Trace a transaction to the GL, then enter your own balanced journal entry

Part 1 — Read a GL impact:

  1. Open any invoice or vendor bill in your sandbox and find its GL Impact view (usually a button or link on the transaction).
  2. Write down the exact accounts debited and credited, and the amounts.

Part 2 — Write a journal entry:

  1. Create a new Journal Entry that moves $500 from one expense account to another (e.g. reclassifying "Office Supplies" to "Software Subscriptions").
  2. Confirm the entry won't save until debits and credits are exactly equal, by intentionally entering mismatched amounts first and observing the error.

Part 3 — Inspect periods (read-only):

  1. Navigate to the accounting periods setup page and identify the current open period and any closed periods before it.
  2. Without closing anything, note what statuses are available for a period (Open, A/P Locked, A/R Locked, Closed, etc., depending on your account's setup).
No live account handy?

Write out, on paper, the debit/credit pair for three different transaction types you've seen so far (an invoice, a vendor bill, a customer payment) — getting the direction of debit vs. credit right for each is the core skill this week is really testing.

5. Knowledge Check

5 quick questions. Expand each to check your answer.

Q1

How does NetSuite "know" an invoice should post to the general ledger — is there a separate step to enter it into the books?

No separate step is needed — the invoice itself is the GL posting. Because sales and accounting share the same database, saving the invoice automatically creates the debit/credit entries against the chart of accounts behind the scenes.

Q2

When would you use a Journal Entry instead of a normal transaction like an invoice or bill?

When an adjustment doesn't come from a normal business event with its own transaction type — accruals, depreciation, reclassifying an expense between accounts, or correcting a miscategorized posting — you enter a direct Journal Entry against the chart of accounts.

Q3

What has to be true about a journal entry's debits and credits before NetSuite will let you save it?

Total debits must exactly equal total credits — the entry has to balance to zero. An unbalanced journal entry can't be saved.

Q4

What does it mean for an accounting period to be "Closed," and why would a finance team want that?

A Closed period no longer accepts new transaction postings into it. Finance teams close periods so that once a month's books are finalized and reported on, the historical numbers stop changing underneath them.

Q5

A EUR invoice is revalued at a lower USD exchange rate than when it was booked. Is the resulting loss realized or unrealized, and when would that change?

It's unrealized — just a paper revaluation of a balance that's still open. It becomes realized once the invoice is actually paid and the foreign-currency cash is actually converted or settled.
