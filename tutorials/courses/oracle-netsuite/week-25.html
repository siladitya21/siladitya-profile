Week 25: Capstone Design: Scoping a Custom NetSuite Solution

Every module from here forward puts tools in your hands — custom records, SuiteFlow, SuiteScript, RESTlets, SDF — and every one of those tools can build the wrong thing just as easily as the right thing if you start typing before you've scoped the problem. This week has no new API surface. It's the planning pass that most NetSuite customizations skip, and the single biggest reason customizations end up fragile, unmaintainable, or quietly duplicating a feature NetSuite already shipped. By the end of the week you'll have a written design for the exact SuiteApp you build and ship in Week 26.

Module 6 of 6 Week 25 of 26 ~3 Hours Design Exercise Included

By the end of this week, you'll be able to

  • Turn a vague business request into written requirements, and map each one to a standard NetSuite object before reaching for a custom one
  • Design a custom record's fields, relationships and sublists on paper before creating a single record type in NetSuite
  • Decide, for each piece of automation, whether it belongs in a SuiteFlow workflow or a SuiteScript — and justify the choice

1. Gathering Requirements & Mapping Them to Standard vs. Custom Objects

A stakeholder rarely hands you a spec. More often it's something like "we need to track equipment loans to employees" or "sales needs to see which deals require legal review before they can be invoiced." Your first job isn't to design anything — it's to turn that sentence into a short, written requirements list: what record is created, who creates it, what data it holds, who approves or acts on it, and what happens when it's done. Writing this down, even in five bullet points, catches ambiguity you'd otherwise discover halfway through a build.

Once you have that list, map every single item against what NetSuite already ships before deciding anything needs to be custom. This ordering matters: building a custom record for something NetSuite already models natively is the single most common mistake in NetSuite customization, and it quietly costs years of maintenance — every report, saved search and integration a standard object gets for free has to be rebuilt by hand for a custom one.

the standard-first checklist
"We need to track equipment loans to employees"

1. Is this an existing standard record with a spin?
   -> Non-inventory/fixed-asset item + a custom transaction form? Maybe.
   -> Does NetSuite Fixed Assets Management (if licensed) already do this? Check first.
2. Is this really a relationship between two EXISTING records?
   -> Could a custom field on Employee + a saved search cover it?
3. Only if 1 and 2 are both "no" -> this earns a custom record type:
   "Equipment Loan" with fields: Employee (list/record), Asset (list/record),
   Loan Date, Expected Return Date, Status
"Custom" should feel like a last resort, not a default

Every week from 2 through 24 taught you a standard object or standard feature precisely so this week you can recognize when one already solves the problem. If you find yourself reaching for a custom record on day one of a scoping exercise, stop and re-check Weeks 2, 5, 6 and 9 first.

A good requirements list for this capstone should fit on one page and answer: who's the primary user, what triggers the process, what data must be captured, who needs visibility into it, and what "done" looks like. That one page is the artifact you'll hand your future self in Week 26 — write it as if someone else has to build it from just that document.

2. Designing the Data Model: Custom Records, Fields & Relationships

Once you've confirmed a custom record is genuinely needed, design it before creating it. Three questions shape almost every custom record: what fields does it need, what type is each field (free text, list/record, date, checkbox — Week 3 covered the full set), and how does it relate to other records — a parent-child relationship, a list/record lookup field, or a sublist on an existing standard record instead of a standalone record type at all.

Relationships are where most designs go wrong. A list/record field creates a simple reference ("this Equipment Loan points at one Employee"). A parent-child relationship (Week 2) nests one record under another and inherits permissions and visibility from it. And sometimes the right answer is no new record at all — just a sublist of line items added to an existing transaction or entity form.

equipment loan — data model sketch
CUSTOM RECORD: Equipment Loan (customrecord_equipment_loan)
+----------------------------------------------------------+
| Field (ID)                  | Type           | Notes      |
|------------------------------|----------------|------------|
| custrecord_el_employee       | List/Record    | -> Employee|
| custrecord_el_asset          | List/Record    | -> Asset   |
| custrecord_el_loan_date      | Date           |            |
| custrecord_el_due_date       | Date           |            |
| custrecord_el_status         | List (custom)  | Out/Returned/Overdue |
+----------------------------------------------------------+
RELATIONSHIP: not parent-child — Employee and Asset are
              REFERENCES via list/record fields, not owners.
Name fields for what a script will call, not just what a form shows

Decide field IDs (custrecord_el_status, not "Status1") at design time. Week 16 onward, your SuiteScript will reference these IDs constantly, and a clear, consistent naming scheme now saves real debugging time later.

Finish the data model by sketching it exactly like the table above — field, type, and a one-line note on what it's for — for every field your capstone needs. That table becomes your literal build checklist in Week 26's Part 1.

3. Planning the Automation: Workflow vs. Script Decision Framework

Once the data model is settled, decide how the record actually behaves — what happens on create, on status change, on a deadline. Weeks 11 and 15–20 gave you two separate toolkits for this: SuiteFlow (no-code) and SuiteScript (code). The capstone is where you have to choose deliberately instead of defaulting to whichever one you remember best.

The decision mostly comes down to three questions: does the logic need to run outside NetSuite's UI (a RESTlet call, a scheduled batch) — that's scripting, full stop, SuiteFlow can't do it. Is the logic simple field updates, approvals and email notifications tied to state transitions — that's SuiteFlow's home turf, and it's visible and editable by an administrator without touching code. Does it need validation, complex calculation, or has to run against governance-heavy bulk data — that's scripting.

decision framework
Does it need to run OUTSIDE the NetSuite UI
(external call in, scheduled batch, cross-record bulk math)?
   YES -> SuiteScript (Scheduled/Map-Reduce/RESTlet — Weeks 18-19)

Is it mostly field updates + approval routing + email,
triggered by a state change on ONE record?
   YES -> SuiteFlow (Week 11)

Does it need custom validation, complex calculation,
or has to touch multiple records atomically on save?
   YES -> SuiteScript (User Event — Week 16)

Can a non-developer administrator need to read or tweak
the logic without opening the SuiteCloud IDE?
   YES -> prefer SuiteFlow, or a documented script with clear comments
Workflow and script aren't mutually exclusive

A SuiteFlow workflow can call a custom SuiteScript action via a "Workflow Action Script" — most real NetSuite builds use both, with SuiteFlow owning the visible process and script handling the one step that's too complex for no-code. Plan for that split now rather than discovering it mid-build.

For the Equipment Loan example: creating the record and routing a manager approval is a SuiteFlow workflow; flagging a loan "Overdue" every night by comparing today's date against hundreds of due dates is a Scheduled Script, because SuiteFlow has no reliable daily batch trigger of its own. Write that same two-column split — "SuiteFlow handles X," "SuiteScript handles Y" — for your own capstone before moving on.

4. Design Exercise

Design Exercise

Scope the SuiteApp you'll build in Week 26

This exercise is pen-and-paper — no NetSuite account required yet. The document you produce here is exactly what you'll follow, step by step, next week.

Part 1 — Pick a process and write requirements:

  1. Pick a small, real business process — equipment loans, a vendor onboarding checklist, an internal purchase-approval log, or one of your own. Keep it small enough to finish in Week 26.
  2. Write a one-page requirements doc: who's the primary user, what triggers the record, what data must be captured, who needs visibility, and what "done" looks like.
  3. For each requirement, note whether a standard NetSuite object already covers it, or whether it genuinely needs a custom record — and write one sentence justifying each "custom" decision.

Part 2 — Design the data model:

  1. Sketch your custom record as a field/type/notes table, exactly like the Equipment Loan example above, including field IDs.
  2. Mark which fields are list/record references to other records, and confirm none of them should actually be a parent-child relationship or a sublist instead.

Part 3 — Apply the automation framework:

  1. List every piece of behavior your process needs (notifications, approvals, status changes, an external integration if relevant).
  2. Run each one through the decision framework and assign it to SuiteFlow or SuiteScript, including a RESTlet if any external system needs to read or write this data (Week 26 will build one regardless, so pick a plausible integration use case even if invented).
Keep it small on purpose

One custom record, one workflow, one script, one RESTlet is plenty for a capstone that proves you can connect every module. Resist scope creep — Week 26 is one more lesson, not a multi-month project.

5. Knowledge Check

5 quick questions. Expand each to check your answer.

Q1

Why should you check standard NetSuite objects before designing a custom record, rather than after?

Standard objects already come with built-in reports, saved searches, workflows and integrations; a custom record has to have all of that rebuilt by hand. Checking first avoids years of avoidable maintenance on something NetSuite already modeled.

Q2

What's the difference between a list/record field and a true parent-child relationship between two records?

A list/record field is a simple reference — one record points at another, nothing more. A parent-child relationship nests one record under another and the child can inherit the parent's permissions and visibility, which is a much stronger coupling than a reference field.

Q3

Why should custom field IDs be decided at design time instead of left as defaults?

SuiteScript references fields by their ID, not their label. A clear, consistent ID scheme chosen up front makes later scripting (Weeks 16 onward) far easier to write and debug than hunting through auto-generated or inconsistent IDs.

Q4

Give one concrete reason a piece of automation MUST be SuiteScript and can't be SuiteFlow alone.

Anything that needs to run outside the NetSuite UI — being called by an external system (a RESTlet) or running as a reliable nightly batch across many records (a Scheduled or Map/Reduce script) — is outside what SuiteFlow can trigger or execute on its own.

Q5

Can a single customization use both SuiteFlow and SuiteScript together? How?

Yes — a SuiteFlow workflow can call a custom SuiteScript action via a Workflow Action Script. This is the common real-world pattern: SuiteFlow owns the visible approval/notification process, and script handles the one step too complex for no-code.
