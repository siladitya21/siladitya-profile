Week 10: Roles, Permissions & Security

Weeks 1–9 treated a role as a given: log in, land on a center, use the records you're allowed to use. This week pulls that apart. A role is a record you design — a list of permissions at a chosen level, a set of restrictions, and (for anything customer- or partner-facing) an automatic boundary around whose data a user can even see. Getting role design wrong is one of the most common ways NetSuite implementations go sideways: too permissive, and anyone can touch the ledger; too restrictive, and people file support tickets just to do their jobs.

Module 3 of 6 Week 10 of 26 ~2.5 Hours Hands-on Exercise Included

By the end of this week, you'll be able to

  • Explain how a role's permission list, level and restrictions combine to control access to a record type
  • Distinguish employee, customer and partner center roles and when each applies
  • Identify segregation-of-duties risks and how to audit role and permission changes over time

1. Role Architecture: Permissions, Restrictions & Level

Open Setup → Users/Roles → Manage Roles → New and a role turns out to be a surprisingly plain record: a name, a center assignment, and a Permissions subtab split into four groups — Transactions, Reports, Lists and Setup. Every permission you add to one of those groups gets a level, and the level is where the real access control lives.

the level ladder
None  <  View  <  Create  <  Edit  <  Full

PERMISSION (Transactions)        LEVEL
Sales Order                      Full      (view, create, edit, delete)
Vendor Bill                      None      (menu item won't even appear)
Journal Entry                    View      (can open and read, not change)

Full is the only level that includes delete — a role with "Edit" on Sales Order can change one but can't remove it. Below the permission grid sits the Restrictions subtab, which narrows access further by data segment: a role can hold "Full" on Sales Order yet still be restricted to a single subsidiary, department, class or location, so a user with that role simply never sees orders outside their assigned segment. The Forms subtab adds one more layer — restricting a role to specific custom transaction forms (Week 14) even when it has permission to the underlying record type.

Grant the narrowest level that still unblocks the job

It's far cheaper to add a permission a user requests next week than to discover, after an incident, that a role's "Full" access let someone delete records they only ever needed to view. Start narrow and widen on request.

2. Employee, Customer & Partner Center Roles

Every role is built on top of a center type, and that choice does more than pick a dashboard layout (Week 1) — it decides who can even be assigned the role and what record ownership rules apply automatically. The three broad families:

center type vs. who logs in
EMPLOYEE CENTER ROLES         CUSTOMER CENTER ROLES        PARTNER CENTER ROLES
----------------------        ----------------------        ---------------------
Assigned to employee          Assigned to a contact on      Assigned to a contact
records; full back-office     a customer record; sees       on a partner record;
access per permissions        only THAT customer's own      sees leads, deals &
above                         orders, invoices, cases       commissions they own

A Customer Center role is how a company lets its own customers self-serve — check an order's status, pay an open invoice, open a support case — without ever touching the Employee Center. A Partner Center role does the same for resellers and affiliates: they see their pipeline, their deal registrations and the commissions NetSuite calculates for them, and nothing of anyone else's.

Crucially, these two role families don't need custom restriction rules to enforce "only my own records" — that boundary is built into the role type itself, driven by which customer or partner record the logged-in contact belongs to. An administrator configuring permissions for a Customer Center role is really configuring what kind of self-service is allowed (view invoices? submit a case?), not whose data is visible — that part is automatic.

Don't try to rebuild "own records only" with custom restrictions on an employee role

If the goal is giving an external party self-service access to their own data, use a Customer or Partner Center role — the ownership boundary is enforced by the platform. Hand-rolling the same restriction on an Employee Center role is fragile and easy to get wrong.

3. Segregation of Duties & Auditing Role Changes

Segregation of duties (SoD) is the principle that no single role should be able to both create and approve the same kind of transaction. NetSuite does not enforce this for you automatically — permission levels happily let you give one role Full access to both creating and approving a vendor bill. SoD has to be designed in, either by splitting those permissions across two roles or by relying on an approval workflow (Week 14) that requires a different person's sign-off before a transaction posts.

a basic SoD conflict matrix
ROLE: AP Clerk          create Vendor Bill   ✓     approve Vendor Bill   ✗
ROLE: AP Manager        create Vendor Bill   ✗     approve Vendor Bill   ✓

If one role could do BOTH, that role alone could invent a bill
and approve its own payment — the exact risk SoD exists to prevent.

Designing SoD once isn't enough, because roles drift — someone adds "just one more permission" to unblock a deadline, and it never gets removed. NetSuite's System Notes subtab tracks field-level changes on individual records, and administrators can build a saved search or SuiteAnalytics workbook over role and permission changes to periodically review what access has crept in since the last audit. Treat that review as a recurring task, not a one-time setup step.

"Who can both create and approve this?" is the single highest-value question to ask

Run it against every financially sensitive transaction type (vendor bills, journal entries, refunds) whenever a role is created or modified — it catches the majority of real-world SoD gaps on its own.

4. Hands-on Exercise

Hands-on

Design and inspect roles in a NetSuite account

Work in a free SuiteTraining account or a sandbox with Administrator access.

Part 1 — Read an existing role:

  1. Open Setup → Users/Roles → Manage Roles and open any standard role, e.g. "Sales Rep."
  2. On the Permissions subtab, record 3 Transaction permissions and their levels.
  3. Check the Restrictions subtab — note whether it's limited to any subsidiary, department, class or location.

Part 2 — Build a narrow custom role:

  1. Create a new role named "AP Clerk — View Only" with View-level access to Vendor Bill and Vendor, and no access to Journal Entry.
  2. Confirm a test user assigned this role can open a vendor bill but cannot see an Edit button.
  3. Note which menu items disappear entirely versus which appear but are read-only.

Part 3 — Compare a self-service role:

  1. Open the standard "Customer Center" role's permission list and compare its scope to the Employee Center role you just built.
  2. Write down, in one sentence, why a Customer Center role doesn't need a subsidiary restriction to keep customers from seeing each other's orders.

5. Knowledge Check

5 quick questions. Expand each to check your answer.

Q1

What's the difference between "Edit" and "Full" level on a permission?

Both allow viewing, creating and changing records; only "Full" additionally allows deleting them. "Edit" stops one level short of delete access.

Q2

A role has Full access to Sales Order but is restricted to the "West" subsidiary. What happens when its user opens the Sales Orders list?

They see only sales orders belonging to the West subsidiary, even with Full permission on the record type — the Restrictions subtab narrows access by data segment independently of the permission level.

Q3

Why doesn't a Customer Center role need a custom restriction to stop one customer from seeing another customer's invoices?

The "own records only" boundary is built into the Customer Center role type itself, driven by which customer record the logged-in contact belongs to — the administrator configures what self-service actions are allowed, not whose data is visible.

Q4

Define segregation of duties in the context of vendor bills, and name one way to enforce it.

No single role should be able to both create and approve the same vendor bill. It can be enforced by splitting create and approve permissions across two different roles, or by requiring an approval workflow with a different approver before the bill posts.

Q5

Why is a one-time SoD review not enough to keep roles safe over time?

Roles drift as permissions get added ad hoc to unblock deadlines and are never removed ("permission creep"). Periodically reviewing role and permission changes via System Notes or a saved search/workbook is needed to catch access that has accumulated since the last review.
