Week 8: Procure-to-Pay: Purchasing & Vendor Bills

Procure-to-pay is order-to-cash's mirror image: instead of a customer buying from the business, the business is the buyer, and a vendor sits on the other side of every transaction. The chain of records looks similar on purpose — a request, a commitment, a receipt, a bill, a payment — but the controls around it matter more, because this is the chain where money actually leaves the company. By the end of this week you'll recognize why approval routing sits at the center of procurement in a way it rarely does for sales.

1. Purchase Requests → Purchase Orders → Item Receipts

Many organizations start procurement with a Purchase Request: an internal "I need this" record, raised by an employee, that doesn't commit the company to anything yet and doesn't go near a vendor. Once approved, it's converted into a Purchase Order — the actual commitment sent to a vendor, with quantities, rates and expected delivery dates on each line.

When goods physically arrive, an Item Receipt records that fact — inventory increases, and like fulfillments on the sales side, an item receipt can be partial if only some of the ordered quantity shows up in a shipment. The purchase order's status tracks this the same way a sales order's status does: "Pending Receipt," "Partially Received," "Pending Billing," and so on.

the procure-to-pay chain
PURCHASE REQUEST  ---(approve)-->  PURCHASE ORDER  ---(receive)-->  ITEM RECEIPT
(internal need,                    (sent to vendor,                 (goods arrived,
 no vendor yet)                     committed)                       inventory +)
                                          |
                                          +----(bill)-->  VENDOR BILL  ---(pay)-->  BILL PAYMENT
                                                           (A/P created,              (A/P cleared)
                                                            GL posts)
Smaller businesses often skip Purchase Requests entirely

If a company doesn't need an internal pre-approval step before anyone talks to a vendor, the chain just starts at the Purchase Order. The Purchase Request record exists specifically for organizations that want a documented "may I buy this" step before any vendor commitment is made.

2. Vendor Bills, Bill Payments & the 3-Way Match

A Vendor Bill is the financial record of what the company owes — it's where accounts payable actually posts to the general ledger, the same way an invoice does on the receivables side. A bill can be entered against a purchase order directly, which pulls in the PO's lines and rates automatically, or entered independently for expenses that never had a PO at all.

The 3-way match is the control that keeps a vendor bill honest: it compares the purchase order (what was agreed), the item receipt (what actually arrived) and the vendor bill (what the vendor is asking to be paid for). If a vendor bills for 100 units but only 80 were received, or bills at a different rate than the PO agreed to, the mismatch is visible before payment goes out — not discovered afterward.

the 3-way match
PURCHASE ORDER   100 units @ $10.00  = $1,000 agreed
ITEM RECEIPT      80 units            received so far
VENDOR BILL      100 units @ $10.00  = $1,000 billed   <-- MISMATCH

  80 received != 100 billed  -->  flag before paying;
  bill should be billed for (at most) the 80 actually received
A bill can be entered without an item receipt existing yet

It's technically possible to bill against a PO before anything has been received, which skips the benefit of the 3-way match entirely. Organizations that care about this control usually enforce, through role permissions or a workflow, that bills can only be entered after a matching receipt exists.

3. Approval Routing on POs & Bills

Because procure-to-pay is where money leaves the business, both purchase orders and vendor bills commonly carry approval routing — a transaction above a certain dollar threshold, or from a certain department, needs a specific person (or a chain of people) to approve it before it can proceed. An unapproved PO can't be sent to a vendor; an unapproved bill can't be paid.

Approval limits are usually tiered by role or by a field on the employee record (an "approval limit" amount), so a manager might approve anything under $5,000 while anything larger escalates to a director. NetSuite can enforce this natively through approval fields on the transaction, or through a full SuiteFlow workflow (which Week 11 covers in depth) when the routing logic needs to branch on more than just a dollar amount.

a simple tiered approval chain
PO amount <= $5,000    -> Manager approval only
PO amount $5,001-$25k  -> Manager -> Director approval
PO amount > $25,000    -> Manager -> Director -> VP approval
"Pending Approval" is its own distinct status

A PO or bill sitting in "Pending Approval" is invisible to downstream processes on purpose — it can't be received against or paid until it clears approval. If a transaction seems "stuck," checking whether it's waiting on an approver is almost always the first thing to look at.

4. Hands-on Exercise

Hands-on

Run a purchase through the procure-to-pay chain, and break the 3-way match on purpose

Part 1 — Order and receive:

  1. Create a Purchase Order for a vendor record with at least one line of quantity 20 or more.
  2. Receive only part of that quantity (e.g. 15 of 20) via an Item Receipt, and confirm the PO's status.

Part 2 — Break the match deliberately:

  1. Enter a Vendor Bill against the PO for the full original quantity (20), not the 15 actually received.
  2. Note what, if anything, your account's configuration surfaces to flag that mismatch — a warning, a field discrepancy, or nothing at all if 3-way match enforcement isn't turned on.
  3. Correct the bill to match the 15 units actually received, and record a payment against it.

Part 3 — Trace approval state:

  1. If your role has approval permissions configured, check whether the PO or bill required approval, and who the approver was.
  2. If not, open Setup and look at how an Approval Limit is configured on an employee record, even without triggering one yourself.

5. Knowledge Check

5 quick questions. Expand each to check your answer.

Q1

What's the difference between a Purchase Request and a Purchase Order, and why would a company use both?

A Purchase Request is an internal "may I buy this" record with no vendor commitment yet; a Purchase Order is the actual commitment sent to a vendor. Companies use both when they want a documented internal pre-approval step before anyone talks to a vendor.

Q2

Which three records does the 3-way match compare, and what is each one supposed to represent?

The Purchase Order (what was agreed), the Item Receipt (what actually arrived), and the Vendor Bill (what the vendor is billing for). A mismatch between any two of them is a signal to investigate before paying.

Q3

A vendor bills for 100 units but only 80 were received on the Item Receipt. What should happen before that bill is paid?

The 20-unit mismatch should be caught and investigated before payment — either the bill is corrected to match the 80 actually received, or the discrepancy is resolved with the vendor, rather than paying for units that never arrived.

Q4

Why does procure-to-pay typically have heavier approval routing than order-to-cash?

Because procure-to-pay is the chain where money actually leaves the business — approvals act as a spending control, making sure purchases above a certain size or from a certain department get sign-off from the right person before the commitment (or the payment) goes through.

Q5

A purchase order is stuck and can't be received against or sent to the vendor. What's the first thing to check?

Check whether the PO is sitting in "Pending Approval." A transaction waiting on an approver is deliberately blocked from downstream actions like receiving or sending to the vendor until it clears approval.
