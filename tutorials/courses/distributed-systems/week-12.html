Week 12: Distributed Transactions

A single-node database gives you an all-or-nothing transaction for free. The moment a "transaction" spans two services that each own their own data, that guarantee has to be built by hand. This week covers the classic, consensus-flavored answer — two-phase commit — the lock-free alternative most real systems actually reach for instead, and the small pattern that makes "update my database and publish an event" atomic without either.

Module 4 of 5 Week 12 of 16 ~3 Hours Hands-on Exercise Included

By the end of this week, you'll be able to

  • Explain why atomicity across two independently-owned resources needs explicit coordination
  • Walk through two-phase commit and explain exactly where and why it can block
  • Design a saga with compensating actions as a lock-free alternative
  • Use the transactional outbox pattern to make a DB write and an event publish atomic

1. Atomic Changes Across Machines

A single-node ACID transaction gives an all-or-nothing guarantee for free: either every change commits, or none do, even across many rows and tables. That guarantee breaks the moment a "transaction" spans two resources that don't share a commit log — for example, debiting an account in one service's database while crediting another account in a second service's database. If the first succeeds and the second fails, money has vanished; there's no single log to roll both back atomically, because there are two independent systems involved, each with its own notion of committed and uncommitted.

This week covers the two broad strategies for this problem: coordinate explicitly so both sides commit or abort together (two-phase commit), or avoid needing atomicity at all by breaking the operation into separately-committed steps with a way to undo them (sagas).

2. Two-Phase Commit & Its Blocking Problem

Two-phase commit (2PC) introduces a coordinator that drives every participant through two rounds:

two-phase commit
PHASE 1 — PREPARE
  coordinator  →  every participant: "prepare to commit"
  each participant: locks the affected rows, writes the change to its own
                     durable log (but does NOT commit yet), replies yes/no

PHASE 2 — COMMIT or ABORT
  if ALL participants voted yes:
    coordinator  →  every participant: "commit"
  else:
    coordinator  →  every participant: "abort"
  each participant: commits (or rolls back) and releases its locks

The critical rule is what a participant promises the moment it votes yes: it must be able to commit no matter what, if later told to — which means it has to keep every affected row locked until the coordinator's decision arrives. That rule is also exactly where 2PC can fail badly: if the coordinator crashes after collecting votes but before sending the decision, every participant that voted yes is stuck holding its locks indefinitely, unable to unilaterally decide to commit or abort on its own (doing so might disagree with what the other participants eventually do). This is 2PC's blocking problem.

The blocking problem is consensus (Week 9) in disguise

A single coordinator deciding commit-or-abort is a single point of failure for that decision — exactly the problem Weeks 9–10 solved by replacing a single decider with a majority vote. Making 2PC itself fault-tolerant means running the coordinator's role as a Paxos/Raft-backed group instead of one node, which is more machinery than most teams want for this problem.

3. Sagas & Compensating Actions

A saga avoids 2PC's locking entirely by giving up atomicity in exchange for something weaker but far cheaper: break the business operation into a sequence of separate local transactions, each committed independently and immediately, and pair every step with a compensating action that undoes it if a later step fails.

a booking saga
STEP 1: reserve flight        commits immediately
STEP 2: reserve hotel         commits immediately
STEP 3: charge payment        FAILS

→ run compensations for the completed steps, in reverse:
  compensate STEP 2: cancel the hotel reservation
  compensate STEP 1: cancel the flight reservation

Sagas can be coordinated two ways: orchestration, where a central saga orchestrator explicitly calls each step and its compensation in order, or choreography, where each service reacts to events from the previous step and emits its own (closer to Week 14's event-driven model). Either way, a saga gives up isolation: between steps 1 and 3 above, another process could see a reservation that's about to be compensated away. Applications using sagas have to be written to tolerate that — showing a "pending" state, or treating compensations as normal, expected outcomes rather than exceptional failures.

4. The Transactional Outbox & Change Data Capture

A narrower but extremely common version of this problem: a service needs to update its own database and publish an event about that update (so other services can react), and those two actions aren't naturally one transaction — the database write and the message-broker publish are two separate systems, exactly like Section 1's two-service problem in miniature.

The transactional outbox pattern sidesteps this instead of solving it head-on: write the event into an "outbox" table in the same local database transaction as the business write — ordinary single-node atomicity covers that part for free. A separate process then reads the outbox table and publishes each event to the broker, retrying as needed (Week 4's idempotency keys make this safe to retry without duplicating effects downstream), and marks the row as published.

transactional outbox — pseudocode
def place_order(db, order):
    with db.transaction():                        # ONE local transaction
        db.insert("orders", order)
        db.insert("outbox", {
            "event": "OrderPlaced",
            "payload": order,
            "published": False
        })

# a separate relay process, running continuously:
def relay_outbox(db, broker):
    for row in db.query("SELECT * FROM outbox WHERE published = false"):
        broker.publish(row.event, row.payload, idempotency_key=row.id)
        db.update("outbox", row.id, published=True)

Change data capture (CDC) generalizes the relay process: instead of polling an outbox table, a tool like Debezium tails the database's own write-ahead log (the same kind of log from Week 5's replication) and turns every row change into an event stream automatically — useful well beyond the outbox pattern, as a way to feed any downstream system from a database's committed changes without the application ever having to "dual-write" at all.

5. Hands-on Exercise

Hands-on

Reproduce 2PC's blocking problem, then solve the same scenario with a saga

Part 1 — Two-phase commit, and a crashed coordinator:

  1. Model a coordinator and 2 participants, each with an in-memory "locked rows" set.
  2. Run a full 2PC round where both participants vote yes and the coordinator sends commit. Confirm locks are released after commit.
  3. Run it again, but have the coordinator crash (stop executing) right after collecting both yes votes, before sending phase 2. Confirm both participants remain locked indefinitely with no way to proceed on their own.

Part 2 — The same scenario as a saga:

  1. Model the same two operations (e.g. "reserve inventory" and "charge payment") as two independent local transactions, each with a compensating action ("release inventory," "refund payment").
  2. Have the payment step fail, and run the compensation for the inventory step. Confirm the system reaches a consistent end state with no indefinite locks anywhere.
  3. Note what's different: for a brief window, the inventory was reserved while the payment was still pending — identify what a real application would need to show a user during that window.

Part 3 — A transactional outbox:

  1. Implement place_order and relay_outbox exactly as in Section 4's pseudocode, using an in-memory "database" and a fake broker that can be told to fail on the first N publish attempts.
  2. Confirm that even if the broker fails repeatedly, the event is never lost (it stays unpublished in the outbox) and is never duplicated once it succeeds (thanks to the idempotency key).
Hint

In Part 1, try having a participant "give up and decide" on its own after a timeout instead of staying blocked. Check what can go wrong if a different participant makes the opposite choice — that's precisely the inconsistency 2PC's blocking is designed to prevent.

6. Knowledge Check

5 quick questions. Expand each to check your answer.

Q1

Why can't a single-node database's transaction guarantee cover an operation that spans two independently-owned services?

Because the two services each have their own independent commit log, and there's no single shared log to atomically roll both back from — a change can succeed in one and fail in the other with no built-in mechanism to undo it.

Q2

What does a participant promise the moment it votes "yes" in 2PC's prepare phase, and what does that promise force it to do?

It promises it will be able to commit if later told to, no matter what. That forces it to keep the affected rows locked until the coordinator's final decision arrives, since it can't risk releasing them and then being unable to honor a later commit instruction.

Q3

Exactly when does 2PC's blocking problem occur, and why can't a participant resolve it on its own?

It occurs if the coordinator crashes after collecting votes but before sending the final decision. A participant can't decide on its own because it doesn't know what the other participants will eventually be told, and unilaterally committing or aborting could disagree with the actual decision.

Q4

What does a saga give up compared to a true distributed transaction, and what does it gain in return?

It gives up isolation — intermediate states between steps are visible to other processes, and failures are handled after the fact via compensation rather than prevented up front. In return, it avoids holding locks across services entirely, removing 2PC's blocking problem.

Q5

Why does the transactional outbox pattern write the event to a local table instead of publishing directly to the message broker inside the same code path?

Because the database write and a broker publish are two separate systems that can't share one atomic commit. Writing the event to a local outbox table lets it ride along in the same local transaction as the business write, which single-node atomicity already guarantees — a separate relay process can then publish it reliably afterward.
