Week 10: Capstone — End-to-End Data Science Project

Time to put the whole tutorial together. You'll pick a real question, gather the data yourself, clean it, analyze it with the statistics and visualization skills from earlier weeks, and deliver a dashboard plus a written report — the same shape as a real data science project.

Phase 4 of 4 Week 10 of 10 ~3–4 Hours Hands-on Exercise Included

By the end of this week, you'll be able to

  • Frame a vague question as a specific, answerable analysis
  • Run a complete project from data collection to a shared deliverable
  • Present findings with honest uncertainty and clear recommendations
1 Framing 2 Project Pipeline 3 Analytical Rigor 4 Deliverables 5 Exercise 6 Knowledge Check

1. Framing the Question

Good projects start with a specific question, a decision it informs, and a way to measure the answer. Turn vague topics into answerable questions:

  • Vague: "Analyze air quality." → Specific: "Did PM2.5 levels in Bengaluru change after the 2025 traffic restrictions, compared with similar cities?"
  • Vague: "Look at the job market." → Specific: "Which skills most often appear in data analyst postings that list higher salaries?"
  • Vague: "Study the library's books." → Specific: "Which genres have the most holds per copy, and where should next year's budget go?"

Write a one-page plan before touching data: the question, the decision it informs, the data sources, the metrics, and what result would change your mind.

  1. Collect raw data and save a dated snapshot.
  2. Clean with a script, logging how many rows each step removes.
  3. Explore visually; write down every surprise.
  4. Analyze: answer the question with appropriate statistics — confidence intervals, a hypothesis test or a forecast, as the question demands.
  5. Communicate: dashboard plus written report.

3. Analytical Rigor Checklist

  • Did you check for duplicates, impossible values and missing data — and document what you did about each?
  • Is the sample representative of the population your question is about?
  • Did you report uncertainty (intervals, not just point estimates)?
  • Have you separated correlation from causation in your wording?
  • Could a confounding variable explain the pattern? Did you check one or two plausible ones?
  • If you compared many groups, did you account for multiple comparisons?
Negative results are results

"There's no meaningful difference between the two campaigns" is a valuable finding — it saves someone from optimizing the wrong thing. Report it as confidently as you would a positive result.

4. Deliverables & Assessment

  • Repository with the layout above and a README explaining how to reproduce every number.
  • Report (2–4 pages): question, data, method, findings, recommendation, limitations.
  • Dashboard: Streamlit app with filters and headline metrics.
  • Five-minute presentation to someone non-technical.

Assess yourself on: clarity of the question, data quality work, correctness of the analysis, honesty about uncertainty, and how quickly a newcomer understands the main finding.

5. Hands-on Exercise

Hands-on

Your capstone project

Choose one of these, or propose your own with the same scope:

  • City air quality: collect PM2.5 readings from a public air-quality API for several Indian cities, analyze trends and seasonality, and compare cities.
  • Job market skills: scrape or collect job postings, extract skills, and test which skills are associated with higher listed salaries.
  • Open-source health: use the GitHub API to compare how quickly issues are resolved across popular data-science libraries.

Milestones: plan (day 1), data collected and cleaned (day 3), analysis complete (day 5), dashboard and report shipped (day 7).

Hint

Scope down aggressively. A narrow question answered rigorously is far more impressive — and more useful — than a broad one answered vaguely.

6. Knowledge Check

4 quick questions. Expand each to check your answer.

Q1

Why write a project plan before collecting data?

It forces a specific question and a definition of success, so you collect the right data and don't drift into aimless exploration. It also gives you a record of what you expected, which keeps your conclusions honest.

Q2

Why should raw data never be edited by hand?

Manual edits aren't recorded and can't be repeated. Keeping raw data untouched and doing all cleaning in code means every result can be traced back and regenerated.

Q3

You find that cities with more parks have lower crime. What should you be careful about in your report?

That's a correlation. Confounders such as income, population density or policing could drive both. Phrase it as an association, mention the plausible confounders, and only claim causation if your design supports it.

Q4

Why report a confidence interval instead of a single number?

A single estimate hides how uncertain it is. The interval tells the reader the range of plausible values, which affects whether a difference is worth acting on.
