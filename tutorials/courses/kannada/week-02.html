Week 2: Greetings, Introductions & Everyday Etiquette

Week 1 gave you the script and a handful of fixed phrases. This week turns those phrases into a real skill: knowing when to use the formal register versus the informal one, introducing yourself properly, asking about someone else, and handling the small courtesies that come up in almost every exchange.

Week 2 of 12 ~2 Hours Conversation Basics Beginner-Friendly

By the end of this week, you'll be able to

  • Choose correctly between Kannada's formal and informal registers
  • Introduce yourself, ask someone's name, and say where you're from
  • Use the everyday courtesy phrases that come up constantly in real conversation

1. Formal vs. Informal: Kannada's Register System

Kannada draws a sharp line between how you address someone familiar and someone you owe respect to — a distinction English mostly lost. The word for "you" itself changes: ನೀನು (nīnu) is informal, used with close friends, peers your own age, and children. ನೀವು (nīvu) is formal/respectful, used with elders, strangers, teachers, and anyone in a position you'd naturally defer to — and it doubles as the plural "you," so it's always a safe default when unsure.

the same question, two registers
Formal (ನೀವು):    ನೀವು ಹೇಗಿದ್ದೀರಾ?     Nīvu hēgiddīrā?     How are you?
Informal (ನೀನು):  ನೀನು ಹೇಗಿದ್ದೀಯ?      Nīnu hēgiddīya?     How are you?

Formal:    ನಿಮ್ಮ ಹೆಸರೇನು?   Nimma hesarēnu?   What is your name?
Informal:  ನಿನ್ನ ಹೆಸರೇನು?   Ninna hesarēnu?   What is your name?

Notice the pattern: the informal forms mostly swap -ī- for -i- and drop a syllable. Once you can hear that shift, you can convert most phrases between registers yourself. When genuinely unsure which to use, default to formal — it's never rude to be too respectful, but the reverse isn't true.

Age, not just familiarity, drives the choice

Even a stranger your own age might get ನೀನು once you're friendly — but someone even a few years older, family or not, typically gets ನೀವು out of respect for age. Watch how locals address each other and you'll pick up the boundary fast.

2. Introducing Yourself & Asking About Others

You already have "Nanna hesaru ___" (my name is ___) from Week 1. This week extends that into a full first exchange: where you're from, and a polite response to being introduced.

a first introduction
ನಿಮ್ಮ ಹೆಸರೇನು?        Nimma hesarēnu?         What is your name?
ನನ್ನ ಹೆಸರು ___         Nanna hesaru ___        My name is ___
ನೀವು ಎಲ್ಲಿಂದ?          Nīvu ellinda?           Where are you from?
ನಾನು ___ ಇಂದ           Nānu ___ inda           I'm from ___
ನಿಮಗೆ ಸಿಕ್ಕಿದ್ದು ಸಂತೋಷ  Nimage sikkiddu santōsha  Nice to meet you (lit. "happy to have met you")

"Nimage sikkiddu santōsha" is worth learning as a fixed phrase rather than analysing word by word — its literal pieces ("to you," "having gotten," "happiness") don't map cleanly onto the English idiom it replaces, which is a pattern you'll meet again and again in this language.

3. Courtesy Phrases & Everyday Etiquette

A small set of phrases carries most of the everyday politeness load in Kannada conversation — learn these well and you'll sound noticeably more natural even with a small vocabulary.

courtesy & everyday etiquette
ದಯವಿಟ್ಟು       Dayaviṭṭu     Please
ಸ್ವಲ್ಪ          Swalpa        A little / just a moment (used to soften requests)
ಪರವಾಗಿಲ್ಲ      Parvāgilla    It's okay / no problem
ಆಗಲಿ           Āgali         Sure / okay (agreeing to something)
ಸ್ವಾಗತ         Svāgata       Welcome
ಇರಲಿ           Irali         That's fine, leave it / never mind

Swalpa deserves special attention — literally "a little," it's added to almost any request to soften it, the way English speakers add "just" ("could you just wait a second?"). "Swalpa dayaviṭṭu kāyiri" (please wait a little) sounds noticeably more polite than "dayaviṭṭu kāyiri" alone, even though the literal meaning barely changes.

"Swalpa" is everywhere

You'll hear "swalpa" constantly in real speech — swalpa help maadi (help a little), swalpa wait maadi. Kannada speakers freely mix in English words like this in casual speech; don't be thrown by it.

4. Hands-on Exercise

Hands-on

Two versions of the same conversation

Write out a short introduction exchange twice — once formal, once informal — to practice switching registers deliberately.

Part 1 — Formal version:

  1. Greet a stranger with Namaskāra, then ask "Nimma hesarēnu?"
  2. Answer with your own name, then ask "Nīvu ellinda?"
  3. Close with "Nimage sikkiddu santōsha."

Part 2 — Informal version:

  1. Rewrite the same three exchanges using ನೀನು forms instead of ನೀವು.
  2. Say both versions out loud back to back, and notice exactly which syllables changed.
Why rewrite instead of just re-reading

Producing both versions yourself — not just recognising them — is what builds the instinct to pick the right register in the moment, which is the actual skill this week is teaching.

5. Practice Quiz

Five questions covering this week's register rules and vocabulary. Attempt each before expanding the answer.

Q1

You're greeting your grandmother for the first time today. Which form of "you" should you use?

A) ನೀನು (nīnu)
B) ನೀವು (nīvu)
C) Either — they're interchangeable
D) Neither — drop the pronoun entirely

Correct answer: B) ನೀವು (nīvu). Elders are addressed with the formal/respectful ನೀವು regardless of how familiar you are with them.

Q2

"Ninna hesarēnu?" is the informal version of which question?

A) How are you?
B) Where are you from?
C) What is your name?
D) What did you do?

Correct answer: C) What is your name? It's the informal ("ninna") counterpart to the formal "Nimma hesarēnu?"

Q3

What does adding "swalpa" to a request mainly accomplish?

A) It makes the request a question instead of a statement
B) It softens and politely downplays the request
C) It changes the tense of the verb
D) It makes the request formal-only, never informal

Correct answer: B) It softens and politely downplays the request. Literally "a little," it functions the way "just" does in English polite requests.

Q4

Which phrase would you use to say "nice to meet you" after an introduction?

A) Parvāgilla
B) Nimage sikkiddu santōsha
C) Swalpa kāyiri
D) Āgali

Correct answer: B) Nimage sikkiddu santōsha. It's the fixed idiomatic phrase for "nice to meet you," used right after an introduction.

Q5

If you're unsure whether to use ನೀನು or ನೀವು with someone, what's the safer default?

A) ನೀನು — informal is always safer
B) ನೀವು — formal is always safer
C) It doesn't matter either way
D) Avoid using "you" entirely

Correct answer: B) ನೀವು — formal is always safer. Being overly respectful is never rude; being too casual with someone who expected respect can be.
