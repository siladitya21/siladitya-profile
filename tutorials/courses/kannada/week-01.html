Kannada Language Syllabus / Week 1

Week 1: The Kannada Script & Pronunciation

Every word you'll ever read or write in Kannada is built from the same small set of vowels and consonants, combined by one consistent system. This week you'll learn to recognise and pronounce the Swaragalu (vowels) and Vyanjanagalu (consonants), see how the Kagunita system combines them into full syllables, and use your first real Kannada words — everyday greetings you can start using immediately.

Week 1 of 12 ~2 Hours Script & Pronunciation Beginner-Friendly

By the end of this week, you'll be able to

  • Recognise and pronounce the 13 Kannada vowels (Swaragalu)
  • Read the main consonant groups (Vyanjanagalu) and build syllables with the Kagunita system
  • Greet someone, introduce yourself, and say thank you and please in Kannada

1. Swaragalu — The 13 Vowels

Kannada is written in its own script, descended from the ancient Brahmi script and shared in structure (though not in shapes) with other South Indian scripts like Telugu. Unlike English, Kannada is largely phonetic — once you know how a letter sounds, it sounds that way almost everywhere you see it, with none of English's silent letters or unpredictable spellings.

Every Kannada syllable is built around a vowel sound, called a swara (ಸ್ವರ). There are 13 of them in everyday use, and they come in short/long pairs — the long version is simply held for roughly twice as long as the short one.

ಸ್ವರಗಳು — the 13 Kannada vowels
ಅ   a    — short, like "u" in "cup"
ಆ   ā    — long, like "a" in "father"
ಇ   i    — short, like "i" in "bit"
ಈ   ī    — long, like "ee" in "see"
ಉ   u    — short, like "u" in "put"
ಊ   ū    — long, like "oo" in "food"
ಋ   ṛ    — a rolled "ri" sound (rare, mostly in borrowed words)
ಎ   e    — short, like "e" in "bed"
ಏ   ē    — long, like "ay" in "say"
ಐ   ai   — like "i" in "kite"
ಒ   o    — short, like "o" in "pot"
ಓ   ō    — long, like "o" in "go"
ಔ   au   — like "ow" in "cow"

Two extra marks are added onto vowels rather than treated as separate letters: the anusvara ಂ, which nasalises a vowel (turns a into aṃ), and the visarga ಃ, which adds a light aspirated "h" sound (a becomes aḥ). You'll see both used constantly once you start reading real words.

Don't memorize by staring

Write each vowel by hand five or six times while saying its sound out loud. Kannada letters are curvy and full of loops that are hard to tell apart by sight alone — your hand learns the shape faster than your eyes do.

2. Vyanjanagalu & the Kagunita System

Consonants (ವ್ಯಂಜನ, vyanjana) are organised into groups called vargas, based on where in the mouth the sound is made — a system inherited from classical Sanskrit phonetics and shared across nearly every Indian script. Learning them by group, rather than as one long random list, makes the pattern click much faster.

the main consonant groups
Kavarga (back of throat): ಕ ka   ಖ kha   ಗ ga   ಘ gha   ಙ nga
Chavarga (palate):        ಚ cha  ಛ chha  ಜ ja   ಝ jha   ಞ nya
Tavarga (retroflex):      ಟ ṭa   ಠ ṭha   ಡ ḍa   ಢ ḍha   ಣ ṇa
Tavarga (dental):         ತ ta   ಥ tha   ದ da   ಧ dha   ನ na
Pavarga (lips):           ಪ pa   ಫ pha   ಬ ba   ಭ bha   ಮ ma

Others:  ಯ ya   ರ ra   ಲ la   ವ va   ಶ sha   ಷ ṣha   ಸ sa   ಹ ha   ಳ ḷa

On its own, every consonant carries an implicit short "a" sound — ಕ is pronounced "ka," not just "k." To attach a different vowel, Kannada doesn't spell out a separate letter; instead it adds a small mark (a matra) to the consonant. This combination system is called Kagunita (ಕಾಗುಣಿತ), and it's the single most important pattern in the whole script — once it clicks, you can sound out almost any word.

Kagunita: combining ಕ (ka) with each vowel
ಕ   ka     ಕಾ  kā     ಕಿ  ki     ಕೀ  kī     ಕು  ku
ಕೂ  kū     ಕೆ  ke     ಕೇ  kē     ಕೈ  kai    ಕೊ  ko
ಕೋ  kō     ಕೌ  kau    ಕಂ  kaṃ    ಕಃ  kaḥ

Notice the pattern: each vowel sign attaches to ಕ in a consistent, predictable position — some marks go before the consonant, some after, some above or below. That same set of marks attaches to every consonant the same way. Learn it once with ಕ, and you can apply it to ನ, ಮ, ಬ or any other consonant without re-learning anything.

Why Kagunita matters more than the alphabet itself

Knowing 13 vowels and roughly 34 consonants only gets you to isolated sounds. Kagunita is what turns them into actual syllables and words — it's worth spending more practice time here than on memorizing the raw letter list.

3. Your First Words: Greetings & Everyday Phrases

With the script's basic shape in view, here are real words you can start using today. Say each one out loud, matching the transliteration to the Kannada script above it — this is exactly the reading skill you'll be relying on for the rest of the course.

everyday greetings & phrases
ನಮಸ್ಕಾರ           Namaskāra          Hello / Greetings
ಹೇಗಿದ್ದೀರಾ?        Hēgiddīrā?         How are you?
ಚೆನ್ನಾಗಿದ್ದೀನಿ      Chennāgiddīni      I'm doing well
ನಿಮ್ಮ ಹೆಸರೇನು?     Nimma hesarēnu?    What is your name?
ನನ್ನ ಹೆಸರು ___     Nanna hesaru ___   My name is ___
ದಯವಿಟ್ಟು           Dayaviṭṭu          Please
ಧನ್ಯವಾದಗಳು         Dhanyavādagaḷu     Thank you
ಕ್ಷಮಿಸಿ            Kṣamisi            Sorry / Excuse me
ಹೌದು               Haudu              Yes
ಇಲ್ಲ               Illa               No
ಶುಭೋದಯ             Śubhōdaya          Good morning
ಬರುತ್ತೇನೆ          Baruttēne          See you (lit. "I'll come")

A couple of these are worth calling out. Namaskāra works as a greeting at any time of day, to anyone, whether you're meeting them or leaving — Kannada doesn't distinguish "hello" from "goodbye" the way English does. And Baruttēne literally means "I'll come" — using it to mean "see you later" is a small piece of the everyday politeness pattern you'll see throughout this course, where the literal words and the actual meaning don't quite match.

Register: formal vs. informal

Hēgiddīrā? is the polite/formal way to ask "how are you," suitable for elders or strangers. Week 2 covers the informal version and how to tell when to switch between the two — for now, the formal version is always a safe default.

4. Hands-on Exercise

Hands-on

Write, sound out, and speak

This exercise has three short parts — do them in order, out loud, on paper if you can.

Part 1 — Write the vowels:

  1. Copy out all 13 vowels from the Swaragalu chart by hand, saying each sound as you write it.
  2. Circle the four pairs that are "short vs. long" versions of the same sound (ಅ/ಆ, ಇ/ಈ, ಉ/ಊ, ಎ/ಏ).

Part 2 — Build your own Kagunita row:

  1. Pick any consonant other than ಕ (try ನ, ಮ or ಬ).
  2. Using the ಕ row as your template, write out that consonant combined with all 13 vowels.
  3. Read your row out loud — if a shape feels unfamiliar, compare it back to the ಕ row and check you placed the vowel mark in the same position.

Part 3 — Speak it:

  1. Say the full greeting exchange out loud: "Namaskāra. Nimma hesarēnu?" then answer your own question with "Nanna hesaru [your name]."
  2. Add "Hēgiddīrā?" and answer it with "Chennāgiddīni, dhanyavādagaḷu."
Why build your own row instead of just re-reading ಕ's

Recognising the ಕ row doesn't prove you understand the pattern — it might just mean you've memorized that one row. Applying the same marks to a different consonant is what actually tests (and builds) the underlying skill.

5. Practice Quiz

Five questions covering this week's script and vocabulary. Attempt each before expanding the answer.

Q1

How many vowels (Swaragalu) are in everyday use in Kannada?

A) 10
B) 13
C) 16
D) 34

Correct answer: B) 13. Kannada has 13 vowels in everyday use, including four short/long pairs and the diphthongs ai and au.

Q2

What sound does a bare consonant like ಕ carry by default, before any vowel mark is added?

A) No vowel sound at all — just "k"
B) A short "a," so ಕ alone is pronounced "ka"
C) A long "ā," so ಕ alone is pronounced "kā"
D) It depends on which word it appears in

Correct answer: B) A short "a." Every consonant carries an implicit short "a" unless a different vowel mark is attached — this is exactly what the Kagunita system modifies.

Q3

What is the name for the system of marks that attaches different vowel sounds onto a consonant?

A) Varga
B) Anusvara
C) Kagunita
D) Visarga

Correct answer: C) Kagunita. Varga refers to a consonant group (like Kavarga); Anusvara and Visarga are the two special nasalisation/aspiration marks — neither is the general combining system.

Q4

Which Kannada word can correctly be used both when meeting someone and when leaving them?

A) Dhanyavādagaḷu
B) Kṣamisi
C) Namaskāra
D) Hēgiddīrā?

Correct answer: C) Namaskāra. Unlike English's separate "hello" and "goodbye," Namaskāra works as a general greeting used on arrival or departure.

Q5

"Nimma hesarēnu?" is best translated as:

A) How are you?
B) What is your name?
C) Where are you from?
D) Thank you very much

Correct answer: B) What is your name? You'd answer it with "Nanna hesaru ___" ("My name is ___"), which pairs directly with this question.
