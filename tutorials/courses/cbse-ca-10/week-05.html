Chapter 5: Cyber Ethics

Based on the official CBSE Computer Applications (165) curriculum, Unit 3 — 10 marks. Having learnt to build and style web pages, this chapter covers the conduct and legal awareness expected of anyone putting content online: netiquettes, software licensing, intellectual property, plagiarism, the digital divide, and staying safe while shopping or banking online.

5.1 Netiquettes

Netiquette ("network etiquette") is the set of unwritten rules for behaving courteously and responsibly online.

Core netiquette rules
- Be respectful; avoid insults, harassment or cyber bullying
- Don't share others' private information/photos without consent
- Avoid ALL CAPS in messages -- it reads as SHOUTING
- Verify facts before forwarding/sharing news or messages
- Don't spam -- avoid repeated unwanted messages or promotions
- Respect others' time and bandwidth -- keep messages/attachments reasonable
- Be careful what you say about others -- it can be screenshotted/forwarded
- Admit and correct your own mistakes gracefully online

5.2 Software Licenses and the Open Source Software Movement

Three broad categories of software license
PROPRIETARY SOFTWARE
  Source code is CLOSED; must usually be purchased.
  Copying/modifying/redistributing is restricted by copyright law.
  E.g. Microsoft Windows, Adobe Photoshop.

FREEWARE
  Free to USE, but source code is still CLOSED (not shared).
  Usually can't be modified or sold commercially.
  E.g. Skype, Adobe Acrobat Reader.

FREE AND OPEN SOURCE SOFTWARE (FOSS)
  Source code is OPEN -- anyone can view, modify and redistribute it,
  usually under a license like the GNU GPL.
  E.g. Linux, LibreOffice, Mozilla Firefox, GIMP.

The open source movement is built on the idea that sharing source code publicly leads to faster bug-fixing, wider collaboration and more innovation, since any capable developer can inspect, improve, or build on existing code.

5.3 Intellectual Property Rights, Plagiarism and Digital Property Rights

Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) — three main forms
COPYRIGHT   protects original creative works (writing, images, music,
            software code) -- granted automatically to the creator
PATENT      protects an INVENTION -- must be formally applied for,
            gives exclusive rights for a fixed period (usually 20 years)
TRADEMARK   protects a BRAND identity -- name, logo, slogan
Plagiarism vs. Copyright Infringement
PLAGIARISM
  Presenting someone else's work/idea as your own -- an ETHICAL
  violation. Even content you're legally allowed to use is
  plagiarism if you don't credit the original author/source.

COPYRIGHT INFRINGEMENT
  Using someone's copyrighted work WITHOUT permission (or without
  paying, if it's sold) -- a LEGAL violation. Crediting the source
  does NOT make it legal if usage itself wasn't authorised.

Digital property rights extend these same ownership protections to digital content specifically — e-books, digital art, software, music files — which can be copied and distributed far more easily (and anonymously) than physical property, making enforcement harder.

5.4 Freedom of Information and the Digital Divide

Two balancing concepts
FREEDOM OF INFORMATION
  The principle that people have a right to access information,
  especially public/government information -- promotes transparency
  and informed citizenship.

DIGITAL DIVIDE
  The gap between those who have ready access to computers/the
  Internet (and the skills to use them) and those who don't --
  due to cost, location, infrastructure, or education. This gap
  can reinforce existing social and economic inequalities.

5.5 E-commerce: Privacy, Fraud, Secure Data Transmission

The three risk areas named in the syllabus
PRIVACY
  E-commerce sites collect personal/payment data -- users should
  check a site's privacy policy and avoid oversharing information.

FRAUD
  Fake shopping sites, phishing emails, and card-skimming attempts
  try to steal payment details. Shop only on well-known, reputable
  sites; verify the seller before paying.

SECURE DATA TRANSMISSION
  Always check for HTTPS (and the padlock icon) before entering
  payment details -- it means the connection is ENCRYPTED, so data
  can't be read if intercepted in transit.
Ties straight back to Chapter 1

The HTTPS protocol from Chapter 1 is exactly the mechanism behind "secure data transmission" here — this is a good example of how the three units of this course (Networking, HTML, Cyber Ethics) connect to each other.

6. Exercise (exam-style, based on the CBSE curriculum)

Unit 3 Practice Questions

Work through these on paper

  1. List any five netiquettes you should follow on a class WhatsApp/email group.
  2. Differentiate between proprietary software, freeware, and FOSS, giving one example of each.
  3. What is the open source software movement, and why does it encourage faster innovation?
  4. Differentiate between copyright, patent and trademark.
  5. Riya copies a paragraph from a website into her project, adds a citation, but never asked the site's permission to reuse it. Is this plagiarism, copyright infringement, both, or neither? Justify.
  6. What is meant by "digital property rights," and how do digital goods differ from physical property in terms of protecting ownership?
  7. What is the "digital divide"? Suggest two steps that could help reduce it in rural India.
  8. You're about to enter your card details on a shopping website. Name two things you should check first to confirm the transaction will be secure.
  9. Differentiate between privacy risk and fraud risk in e-commerce, with an example of each.
  10. Why does HTTPS matter specifically for e-commerce transactions?
